# Editorial taste assessment

Use when judging whether copy is good, resonates, or deserves to be selected.
Check facts and format separately through [assessment](assessment.md). Passing
those checks does not establish strong editorial quality.

## Establish the intended feel

Use the brief's audience, situation, purpose, and desired impression. Describe
observable choices: "warm and firm, without lengthy self-defense" or "confident
through detail, without inflated claims". Use liked/disliked samples and reasons
when supplied. Otherwise assess provisionally from context; ask only when the
choice changes direction, not for every small preference.

An explicit user preference anchors the intended feel for related work, even
when a reviewer previously scored another version higher. Reassess affected
dimensions against that reference with quotes and reasons; do not inflate every
score to make the selected version win. Facts and format remain separate checks.

## Six dimensions

Score each dimension 1-10 as editorial judgment, not objective taste measurement:

- 1-2: misses; the writing needs a different direction.
- 3-4: some choices work, but weaknesses dominate.
- 5-6: readable and adequate, yet flat or generic.
- 7-8: good for this brief; remaining edits are local.
- 9: strong, deliberate choices supported by quotations.
- 10: exceptional for this brief; identify the specific advantage over merely
  strong writing. This is not universal perfection.

Use integers. Choose within a band by severity and positive evidence; explain
borderline judgments. High scores are not a quota. Do not sum or average. Mark
insufficiently evidenced dimensions unassessable rather than assigning zero.

| Dimension | Question | 1 - Misses | 9-10 - Strong in context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reader relevance | Does it enter through something that matters to this reader? | Starts with writer priorities or interchangeable promotion. | Chosen situations/details show understanding without pretending to know readers' lives. |
| Sharpness of perspective | What message is chosen, and what is left out? | Feature list, safe filler, or competing messages with no focus. | One clear idea and purposeful order make the message worth attention. |
| Credible voice | Do choices reveal appropriate care or perspective? | Generic phrasing with pronouns or slang substituting for character. | Grounded detail, attention, or reasons convey a voice without invented stories. |
| Reading pleasure | Does the next sentence feel worth reading? | Mechanical rhythm, repetition, or forced drama obstructs. | Connections, pauses, and emphasis make the writing easy to follow without a template rhythm. |
| Judgment and restraint | Is warmth, persuasion, emotion, and explanation proportionate? | Overselling, coldness, forced intimacy, defensiveness, or needless explanation. | Appropriate stance and emphasis, with enough information and a well-chosen stopping point. |
| Intended effect | Does the desired response feel earned by the writing? | Bolted-on CTA, indistinct takeaway, or unclear stance. | Choices support the goal: an offer worth considering, a clear decision, or an easy next step. |

Match the standard to the task. A WhatsApp refusal needs clarity and relationship
care, not an ad hook. Skill instructions help an agent make the next decision;
they need not entertain. Official notices need not be distinctive. Without brand
samples, distinguish provisional task-fit from unverified brand resemblance.

## Evidence and decision

For each dimension quote the decisive text, explain its effect for the specified
reader, and identify one valuable edit if needed. "Clean", "natural", "AI-like",
or "I like it" alone is insufficient. Separate preference from brief violations.
Check sources before claiming an invented detail; audience context need not
always be printed literally.

Compare versions without model labels where possible. Select based on intended
feel and purpose, with decisive quotes and tradeoffs. Say tied if tied. Shorter,
funnier, or more personal is not automatically better. Never invent facts to
improve taste.

Conclude with an editorial verdict: **misses the intended feel**, **usable but
flat**, or **worth selecting for this brief**, with decisive reasons rather than
a score formula. Predicted effect is not observed audience response or sales.
Factual defects still prevent declaring copy ready to use even when taste is strong.

## Example: correct versus compelling

Illustrative material: lunch catering for employees around Tebet, Rp35,000 for
rice, grilled chicken, and two vegetables. Assess openings only, not full captions.

A: "I offer a Rp35,000 lunch package with rice, grilled chicken, and two vegetables."
B: "Working around Tebet and looking for lunch? I prepare rice, grilled chicken,
and two vegetables in one Rp35,000 package."

Both preserve facts. For new Instagram readers B leads with their situation;
A announces the offer directly. B does not establish a distinctive brand voice.
If the visual already identifies reader and location, A may be more efficient.
This is contextual editorial reasoning, not a universal winner or proof of sales.
